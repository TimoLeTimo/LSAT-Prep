### Passage rc-passage-000001

When an archive receives a person's papers, its staff must decide whether to keep them in the arrangement in which they arrived or to sort them by subject. Defenders of the first practice hold that arrangement is itself evidence. A letter filed among household bills, they point out, may reveal something about how its recipient regarded it that the letter's words do not. Sorting by subject destroys such clues, and because nobody can know in advance which clues future researchers will want, the loss cannot be justified by present convenience.

Critics reply that this argument assumes what it needs to show: that the arrangement reflects the owner's choices. Papers are often moved before they reach an archive. Relatives empty desks in haste, movers fill boxes by size, and a flood on one shelf may send its contents to another. An order produced by such events is evidence of those events, not of its owner's habits of mind. A researcher who reads intention into it will be misled, and misled more thoroughly for believing the arrangement to be untouched.

The critics' observation is sound, but the conclusion some of them draw, that archives may as well sort by subject, does not follow. That an arrangement might mislead is a reason to record how it came about, not a reason to erase it. An archivist who notes that certain boxes were packed by a nephew gives researchers the means to discount what the nephew did; an archivist who sorts the boxes removes both the misleading order and whatever genuine order survived beneath it. Nor does sorting spare researchers from the influence of someone's intentions, since a subject scheme embodies the choices of the person who devised it.

The real cost of preserving arrangement is labor. Researchers who want every letter about a single topic must search widely for them. Where searchable descriptions of individual items can be made, this cost diminishes, because the same papers can then be found by subject while remaining physically as they were. Where such descriptions cannot be afforded, an archive may reasonably sort some collections, provided that it first records the order it is about to disturb.

## Part 2 — Key and explanations

### lr-weaken-000001: B

- A. Incorrect. How long visitors stay says nothing about whether they used the library before the hours changed.
- B. Correct. The conclusion is that the longer hours brought in people who had not used the library before. The evidence shows only that people come in the evening. If most of them were already regular afternoon visitors, the evening attendance is largely existing users changing the time of their visits, which removes most of the support for the conclusion.
- C. Incorrect. Cost bears on whether the longer hours are worthwhile, not on whether they attracted new users.
- D. Incorrect. This does not show that the weekday evening visitors had used the library before. If anything, it rules out one other change as a factor.
- E. Incorrect. Attendance elsewhere does not show whether this library's evening visitors are new. A library can draw more visitors than others and still draw the same people as before.

### lr-necessary-assumption-000001: D

- A. Incorrect. The conclusion concerns cracking, not cost. The argument is unaffected if the electric kiln is more expensive to run.
- B. Incorrect. This is stronger than the argument needs. Some slowly cooled pieces could crack for other reasons while the proportion that crack still falls.
- C. Incorrect. The argument needs only a difference in how the kilns are in fact used. Even if the gas kiln could have been adjusted to cool slowly, the proportion could fall if it was not used that way.
- D. Correct. The only reason offered for expecting less cracking is that the electric kiln can be set to cool slowly. If the workshop will not use that setting for any glazed piece, the kiln's ability to cool slowly gives no reason to expect a change, and the argument fails.
- E. Incorrect. The conclusion is about the proportion of pieces that crack. That proportion could fall whether the number fired goes up, goes down, or stays the same.
